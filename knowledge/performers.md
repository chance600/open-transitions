### James Zabiela — the FX technician

The original FX-technician: Pioneer EFX-1000/DJM beat-FX composition (feedback loops, beat rolls, transform effect), live sampling of vocals through the mic input, polyrhythmic pivot loops for tempo modulation. His Pioneer DJ "How To DJ" 8-part series (2009) has controller-cam close-ups of the feedback loop, beat roll, and transform moves; the NXS2 trick videos cover Vinyl Brake acid noise and Helix sampling. Because he teaches on camera, he is an L4 (performer-confirmed) source — the calibration standard for the whole reverse-engineering pipeline.
Phase 22 (expansion tranche 1, 2026-10-22) mined the tutorial series at episode-description weight — all eight parts of Episode 3, his own teaching: Part 1, effects + how to set up a feedback loop; Part 2, *editing* feedback loops — halving the set BPM to change the loop's length, spinback fills inside the held loop, sampling from the CDJ into the EFX; Part 4, the set-closing "thank you" vocal mashup — headphones plugged into the mixer's mic input to record his own live vocals into the EFX-1000's sampler, layered with extra looped sounds (Kaossilator hi-hats); Part 5, the DJM-800 beat roll (+ reverse roll) used as a sampler; Part 7, scratch/transform craft incl. a feedback loop of his own scratching; Part 8, the transform effect on a looped scratch sample, patterns improvised on the beat-select buttons. Banked from this pass: `transform_gate_loop` implemented (below); the BPM-halving loop-length change recorded as the **loop_length_modulation** axis on `feedback_loop_build`; the live-vocal capture recorded as the **capture_source: live mic** axis on `live_loop_mix` (DM-A14) — the sample source is the room itself, which is why the move closes sets. Balance 029's composite staging is banked in knowledge/setcraft.

Footage: behind-the-decks clips, the 4-deck London Underground set (2020), Brooklyn Mirage 2022 transition clips, recent 16-deck rig demos (stem-strip/layering arrangement craft). The rare performer who also teaches: his Digital DJ Tips "James Hype's Mixing Skills" course is ground truth for calibrating extraction.

### Laidback Luke — the quick-mix manipulator

Signature: density and manipulation as the style itself — "I hardly ever play a track from start till finish, I love manipulating the music and that keeps me busy during a DJ set"; a Mixmash Year Mix at "about 60 tracks in one hour." His named-mix taxonomy (his Digital DJ Tips course curriculum) is a performer's own transition catalog: the "One Climax" mix, drop mixing, "Here Comes The Drop," "Scratch Drop," "Bait & Switch," triplet-track DJing, "Delay Doubles," fader fills, acapella loop transitions, "Laidback Loops With Riffs," and wordplay — most resolve onto registry bases/compounds (see TECHNIQUES Phase 12); the names are his, the mechanisms the registry's. Two doctrines travel with the taxonomy: tempo and energy are independent variables (the "Tempo Rollercoaster" is ridden deliberately, not survived), and genre switches are made by "Element Matching" — a shared element carries the seam when tempo and key can't. Scratch competence is his insurance policy, stated as recovery craft: "if you can scratch, you can be more confident behind the decks, because when something goes wrong for some reason you can always recover by switching the track & making sure it's playing at the correct number of beats per minute" — manual skill as the substrate of the salvage repertoire (knowledge/setcraft, trainwreck salvage). His Dancefair "Real DJing" workshop (2015) teaches the same system publicly: looking for similarities between songs, creating live mashups with acapellas.
Provenance: Festivalling interview (verbatim quotes), https://www.festivalling.com/interviews/interview-with-laidback-luke/ ; Digital DJ Tips, "Laidback Luke Teaches Mixing" course curriculum (lesson titles), https://milled.com/learn-to-dj-with-virtual-dj-home-online-video-course/54-mix-techniques-33-lessons-1-big-transformation-sgYpVB67ambzz-by ; DJcity News, Dancefair workshop topic breakdown, https://news.djcity.com/tag/laidback-luke/page/2/ — mined 2026-10-21 (study program tranche 7).

### DJ Angelo — the turntablist as transitionist

A world-champion turntablist whose teaching canon is the registry's scratch/juggle evidence base: his Turntable Tutorial Collection sequences the vocabulary in the order the art teaches it — basics, tear (faderless), stab, transformer, chirp, one- and two-click flare orbits, crab, then beat juggling (basics, with loops, with pauses) — and his Digital DJ Tips course ("Tricks & Transitions") is built on the premise the registry shares: scratch and juggle techniques are *transition* tools for club sets across genres and tempos, not battle ornaments. The course's named forms (acapella switch/bridge, one-deck quick-switch, beat chasing/teasing, sliding tone play, tone play with a huge key shift) resolve onto registry compounds (TECHNIQUES, Phase 13); his own advanced names (Autobahn, Boomerang, Turntable Drumming) are recorded name-only, mechanisms unpublished in free text. His 4PLAY routine — four turntables, "understated yet technically complex," deliberately *not* "mega clicky fader action" — is the deck-count extreme of the juggle/layer families performed with restraint: four-deck turntablism as flow, the controllerism-era proof that the scratch canon scales by decks the way the layer family scales by stems (study program tranche 8, 2026-10-21).

### What the literature recovers — and where it stops

ISMIR 2020 (Kim/Yang/Nam): transition regions, lengths in beats, tempo/key deltas — no gains, no FX. NIME 2021: per-band gain trajectories + control-type classification — FX appear only as reconstruction error. CMMR 2019 (Schwarz & Fourer): placement, time-stretch, fade curves on synthetic mixes (UnmixDB). The papers stop at gain curves; this library's pipeline adds the FX moves, loop/juggle events, the gesture layer, and the why/when-not interpretation.

### A-Trak — risk as obligation, legibility as constraint

A-Trak's performer doctrine, in his own words across two decades: the set must visibly attempt things — "When you come to my show you know you'll see me cut. And take risks. DJing is about taking risks" (#RealDJing, 2014) — and the attempts must be *understood by the room in front of him*: rebuilding his battle routines for Kanye West's arena audiences, "My routines were too specialized so I had to make new ones that this new audience would understand" (DJ Times, 2007). The pair is the counterweight to the restraint doctrines banked from Luke and Angelo: restraint governs *density*; A-Trak's rule governs *ambition and address* — a transition vocabulary that never risks failure reads as button-pushing, and one the audience cannot parse reads as noise. His craft premise is the notation method (co-invented, late 1990s): scratch patterns are discrete enough to write down, which is why his routines are compositions — scored, rehearsed, and then risked live (study program tranche 9, 2026-10-21).
Provenance: A-Trak, #RealDJing post via DJ TechTools, 2014-10-23, https://djtechtools.com/2014/10/23/are-you-really-djing-a-trak-and-the-real-djing-debate/ ; DJ Times interview, Jan 2007; Fusicology interview, 2010 — read first-hand 2026-10-21.

### Rob Swift — patterns are discovered, and they are social

Rob Swift (X-Ecutioners) teaches turntablism as a lineage with a discovery epistemology: "Every 'Beat Juggle' pattern you can possibly coax out of two turntables exists already… you determine the discovery of these patterns by the ways in which you move" — the record contains the pattern space; the practitioner's movement (and mindset) selects from it, which is why his advice to students is anti-formulaic ("Stop approaching Beat Juggling like a math equation… explore the records. Get curious") and drummer-framed ("approach it like you're a drummer"; his Cardinal Rules import the Single Paradiddle as a scratch rudiment). His ensemble doctrine, Scratch Synchronicity, adds the social constraint the solo literature omits: in a multi-DJ jam, "you must be mindful of how your scratch execution is making everyone else sound. Are your scratch patterns making the person(s) you're scratching with sound better or worse?… adjust your scratches accordingly" — technique judged by its effect on the *other* performers, the scratch family's version of the restraint doctrine (study program tranche 9, 2026-10-21).
Provenance: DJ TechTools, "5 Powerful Lessons from Rob Swift's Turntablism Classes", 2017-04-14, https://djtechtools.com/2017/04/14/5-powerful-lessons-rob-swifts-turntablism-classes/ ; Rob Swift, "A Beginner's Guide to Backspinning" + "Don't Think, Feel", https://www.djrobswift.com — read first-hand 2026-10-21.

### DJ Jazzy Jeff — mastery ends, invention begins; learn everyone's techniques, use them your own way

Jeff's 1988 self-assessment states the performer's two-phase doctrine: "I can do everything there is to be done on turntables. The turntables are mastered. Now it's just a process of making up new techniques" — execution mastery is a *completed* phase; the standing work is invention (his chirp is the doctrine's artifact, below). His study method is this program's own, stated by a practitioner a generation early: "You have to be able to mix without equipment. You have to be able to think of mixes, think of things that might work together, while you're sitting down eating your dinner. Examine everybody else's techniques and learn from them. Incorporate those techniques into your own mixes, but use them in your own way" — technique acquisition is off-deck and mental first, incorporation is mandatory, imitation is not the endpoint. His Playlist Retreat (his house as a working school for elite DJs, covered by Rolling Stone in 2024) is the doctrine's institutional form — the study group as a residence — recorded at headline weight: the article itself is blocked in this environment and stands unread (study program tranche 10, 2026-10-21).
